What would actually help

Mario Draghi's report on the future of European competitiveness, commissioned by the European Commission and published in September 2024, is the shared starting point for what follows. This page is not that report. It names three of the frictions it points to that bear directly on founding, funding and scaling a company in Germany, and the concrete instrument — enacted, proposed, or still contested — attached to each. None of this is a party-political argument; it is about what would let more companies like these exist.

Formation friction

Until 2022, forming a GmbH in Germany meant an in-person notary appointment, with no digital alternative. The Gesetz zur Umsetzung der Digitalisierungsrichtlinie (DiRUG), implementing an EU directive, changed that: since 1 August 2022 the notarisation can happen over video call, trade-register extracts are free, and a company's details are submitted once rather than re-entered into separate registers and announcement portals — real, working digital government, recorded in the Federal Ministry of Justice's own announcement.

It is not the ceiling. The European Commission's own case for its next reform states that a company today still faces 27 national legal systems and more than 60 company forms the moment it expands across an EU border — fragmentation the Commission says can delay setting up a company by weeks or months. Its own account of the problem is blunt about the scale of it. Germany's 2022 reform proves the fix — cheap, fast, digital, no repeated paperwork — works domestically. Extending that logic across a border is not something Germany can do alone.

No single European home

A GmbH is a German company, not a European one. A founder raising from investors across the EU, hiring across borders, or moving headquarters later still has to deal with 27 separate company-law systems, each with its own registry and insolvency rules. That fragmentation is what a coalition of founders and investors — more than 26,000 of them, and still growing, by the campaign's own count — has spent since late 2024 petitioning the European Commission to fix, under the banner EU Inc, after Delaware's single, portable corporate form for the US.

On 18 March 2026 the Commission answered with a formal proposal: an optional, digital-by-default EU company form — the "28th regime," alongside the 27 national ones — offering registration within 48 hours for under €100 with no minimum share capital, EU-wide employee stock option plans, and simplified insolvency procedures. It arrives as a draft regulation, and the Commission has asked Parliament and the Council to reach agreement by the end of 2026, with a stated ambition of a single European market for companies by 2028. The Commission's announcement sets out the full proposal.

This is a proposal, not law, and the reception has been mixed. Advocacy groups including Allied for Startups and the European Startup Network have publicly warned the draft falls short of a true 28th regime. Lucien Burm, chair of the Dutch Startup Association, put it more specifically: it still relies too much on 27 national systems and legal cultures to count as a genuinely European company. A separate concern, from Eldian Biomed co-founder Gregor Bordon, is that registration and disputes may still be tied to national registries and courts, short of the simplification founders and investors had pushed for. Whether the version that reaches agreement resembles the proposal or the petition is a live legislative question, not a settled one.

A gap in growth capital

The two frictions above matter most to a company being founded. What decides whether it stays independent and German while it scales, rather than raising a late round from a fund abroad, is whether growth-stage capital exists here at all. Germany's Zukunftsfinanzierungsgesetz, passed by the Bundestag in November 2023, is aimed at that stage specifically: it eased the tax treatment of employee share schemes — the tax-free allowance for employee capital participation now stands at €2,000 a year — and lowered some of the regulatory cost of going public, making it more attractive for a company's own employees and investors to stay in for the long stretch.

Alongside that legal groundwork sits actual capital. The WIN-Initiative, launched in September 2024 by the federal government, KfW and a coalition of companies and associations, aims to mobilise around €12 billion in growth and innovation capital for young companies by 2030; by the end of 2025 it reported €2.64 billion committed. At EU level, the Commission's Savings and Investments Union strategy, published in March 2025, is the broader attempt to do this at the scale the Draghi report says is needed — an additional €750-800 billion a year — by connecting European savings to exactly the kind of company this register tracks. None of these three instruments is finished. Together, they are the difference between a founder who has to leave for capital and one who does not have to.
